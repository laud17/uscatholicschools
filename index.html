Catholic education in the United States

1.7 million students rely on a workforce we know too little about.

Catholic schools form the nation’s largest private-school network and its second-largest school sector after public education. This project tracks the teachers who make that system possible.

The central finding

Teachers are committed to their schools. Their working conditions tell a harder story.

National survey estimates show high satisfaction with Catholic schools and strong support from administrators. They also show long workweeks, low salary satisfaction, and limited influence over school decisions.

Catholic school teachers, 2020–21

Commitment and pressure coexist.

More than nine in ten teachers were generally satisfied at their school. Fewer than four in ten were satisfied with their salary. Teachers reported 50.3 hours of work in a typical week, including 37.0 hours covered by base pay.

An experienced workforce

The average Catholic school teacher was 45.9 years old and had 15.0 years of teaching experience.

A succession question

49.3% planned to remain as long as they were able. Another 18.3% were undecided.

03

A pay gap within private education

Average Catholic school base salary was $43,102, about $10,300 below the nonsectarian private-school average.

04

A long workweek

Catholic teachers reported 50.3 weekly hours, more than teachers in the other private-school sectors.

What changed

The workforce is older, more diverse, and under pressure.

The series begins with early SASS point estimates and continues through the 2020–21 NTPS. Gaps remain where measures changed or data were not available.

Demographic change

The workforce aged, then began to diversify.

The share of Catholic teachers age 50 or older rose sharply in the early SASS years. Since 2003–04, the shares of male teachers and teachers of color have increased, though both groups remain a minority of the workforce.

Autonomy

Classroom control remains high, but one measure fell.

Teachers continued to report substantial control over teaching techniques. The share reporting a great deal of control over student discipline fell from 73.6% in 2003–04 to 53.3% in 2020–21.

Teacher preparation

Content preparation outpaces classroom preparation.

In 2007–08, 86.0% felt well prepared to teach their subject matter. Only 61.0% said the same about classroom management.

The autonomy split, 2020–21

Teachers control their classrooms, not the institutions around them.

Classroom practice and school governance are different forms of professional authority. Catholic teachers reported far more control over instruction than influence over hiring and budgets.

Methods and limitations

Survey coverage

Following teachers from SASS to NTPS.

The archive combines early pilot trends, validated estimates for 2003–04 through 2011–12, partial 2017–18 results, and a broad 2020–21 release. Measures are connected only when wording and response categories support comparison.

Read a line carefully

A break in a chart may reflect a changed survey item or unavailable data. Early 1987–2000 estimates are provisional until their replicate-weight variance method is verified.

Methods

Every estimate carries its limits.

The site reports weighted national survey estimates. It preserves standard errors, confidence intervals, and source flags when available. These data describe teachers and their working conditions. They do not estimate effects on student achievement.

Nationally weighted

Teacher weights define the population represented by each estimate.

Uncertainty retained

Standard errors, confidence intervals, suppression flags, and reliability warnings stay with the data.

Trends reviewed

Question wording and response categories are checked before estimates are compared.

Claims kept narrow

Cross-tabulations are associations unless a research design supports a causal claim.
